What one request costs · Lesson 03

Numbers you must be able to estimate

"Will it hold on launch day?" is a question with a numerical answer, and the arithmetic takes a minute. A dozen constants, four multiplications and one honest rounding — and the design's hard part introduces itself before a line of it is written.

01Ground floor

A guess and an estimate look the same and are not

TAPAny word with a dotted underline opens a plain explanation in English and Vietnamese, plus where that thing shows up once a system is actually running. Nothing here assumes you have met it before.

Somebody in a meeting says "we'll need a big database". Somebody else says "we should be fine on one server". Both sound like engineering. Neither is, yet — they are guesses, and the difference between a guess and an estimate is not confidence or experience. It is whether the inputs were written down and multiplied.

An estimate starts from one number almost every product has: . It multiplies that by what a user does in a day — how many things they create, how many they look at — and by how big each of those things is. Four results fall out, and they are the same four for every system ever designed: writes a second, reads a second, bytes a year, and bits a second leaving the building. Then each of the four is held up against what one machine, or one network link, can do. The one that is furthest past its limit is the hard part. Sometimes none of them is, and that is the most useful answer of all.

The whole thing is a minute of arithmetic, and it is worth more than an hour of architecture talk, because it turns "we'll need a big database" into "we'll write two hundred rows a second and store forty terabytes a day — the database is fine and the disks are the problem". Those are different projects. Only the arithmetic can tell you which one you are on.

In productionEvery one of those four numbers has a graph once the system runs: requests a second on the load balancer, split by method; bytes on the storage dashboard; the egress line on the cloud bill. The estimate is a prediction of those four graphs. Comparing it to them a month after launch is how you find out whether you can estimate — and how your next estimate gets better.

02The idea

A dozen constants, and the discipline of rounding

There is a short list of numbers that make estimation possible, and it is short enough to know by heart. Not to three digits — to the power of ten. Every one of them is a ratio waiting to be used: how much slower a disk is than memory, how much slower a network is than a disk, how many seconds a day has.

  • Main memory read — ~100 nanosecondsThe baseline. Everything below is "how many times slower than this".
  • SSD random read — ~100 microsecondsA thousand times slower than memory. Tens of thousands of them a second — the figure.
  • Round trip inside a datacenter — ~0.5 millisecondsFive thousand memory reads. The price of every call to another machine, before that machine does anything.
  • Spinning disk seek — ~10 millisecondsA hundred times an SSD. A few hundred a second at most, which is why nobody puts a hot database on one any more.
  • Round trip across a region — ~30 ms · across an ocean — ~150 msLesson 02. The only numbers here that no purchase order can change.
  • Read 1 MB — memory ~0.1 ms · SSD ~1 ms · 1 Gbps network ~10 msSize times medium. The reason a 2 MB photo costs more to serve than a 200-byte row costs to store.
  • Seconds in a day — 86,400, call it 100,000Per-day to per-second is "divide by a hundred thousand". 20 million a day is 200 a second. Close enough, every time.
  • A thousand, a million, a billion — KB, MB, GB; a trillion — TBPowers of a thousand, and 210, 220, 230 are close enough to them that the difference never changes a design.
  • One well-run machine — a few thousand simple requests a second · a few hundred writes · about 10 TB of diskRound on purpose. If your number is ten times one of these, you have found the hard part; if it is a tenth, you have not.

With those in hand, the method is four steps and one habit. Write the inputs down — users, actions per user, size per action — and label each as measured or assumed. Multiply to get the four outputs. Multiply the rates by two or three for , because traffic is not spread evenly across a day and a launch is not a typical day. Compare each output to one machine. And the habit: round everything to one significant figure as you go. 20 million uploads over 86,400 seconds is 231.48 a second, and every digit after the 2 is a lie about how well you know the inputs. Say two hundred. The is the answer; the rest is noise.

Two of the four outputs need a size to exist, and the size is where estimates most often go wrong by a factor of ten. A row with a few ids and timestamps is a couple of hundred bytes; a row with a text field is a kilobyte or more; a thumbnail is a couple of hundred kilobytes; a photo from a phone is two to five megabytes. The decides whether bytes are the hard part, and it is the input most worth checking against a real table before trusting.

The last output is the one engineers forget and finance departments do not. Reads a second times bytes per read is , and it is quoted in bits, so multiply by eight. Seventeen thousand thumbnail views a second at 200 KB each is 3.4 GB a second — 27 gigabits — out of the door, every second, at peak. No single link carries that, and even if one did, egress is the most expensive line on most cloud bills. The arithmetic that says "the request rate is modest" is the same arithmetic that says "we need a CDN", if you carry it one step further.

An estimate without its inputs written down is a rumour with a number in it.

One more multiplier belongs in the kit. Not every item is read equally: a fifth of them get four fifths of the traffic — the . That is why a cache a tenth the size of the data can answer most requests, and it is how to size one: a day's worth of new items, times a fifth, times the size of each. And bytes a year is only a number on a disk once you know how many years — is a business decision, legal or product or cost, that the estimate has to ask about rather than assume.

In productionThe one-machine limits above are the reason a good design doc's capacity section ends in zeros: "~200 writes/s, ~6k reads/s, ~15 PB/yr". A reviewer can check each of those in their head against the constants. A doc that says 231.48 writes a second is telling you its author did not understand that the inputs were guesses.

03The danger

Four ways an estimate lies, and one sanity check

Estimates go wrong in a small number of repeatable ways, and none of them is arithmetic. They are all about what was multiplied, what was forgotten, and how the answer was dressed.

Precision theatre

"We will receive 231.48 uploads per second." The inputs were a guess at daily users and a guess at uploads per user, each probably wrong by half. Five significant figures on the product of two guesses is not rigour; it is a costume. Worse, it invites the reader to treat the number as measured, and to build to it. Round to one figure and say so — "about two hundred, could be a hundred or four" — and the estimate is honest about what it knows.

The average day that never happens

Dividing by 86,400 gives the rate on a perfectly flat day, and no day is flat. Evenings are busier than mornings; a sale, a launch or a news story can be ten times a normal hour. Design for two to three times the average as a matter of routine, and ask what the one-day-a-year peak looks like before deciding it is somebody else's problem. A system sized for the average is a system that fails on its most important day.

The unit that quietly changed

Per day where per second was meant. Megabytes where megabits were meant — a factor of eight, in the direction that makes the network look fine. Kilobytes where megabytes were meant, because "the photo is about 2,000" and nobody said of what. Every one of these is a factor of ten or more, and every one has shipped. Write the unit next to every number, including the intermediate ones, and the error has nowhere to hide.

The size nobody checked

"A row is about 100 bytes" — and then the row has a JSON column, and it is 4 KB, and the storage estimate was off by forty. The size of one thing is the input that is easiest to check and most often assumed. If a table exists, ask it for its average row size. If an object store exists, ask it for its average object. If neither exists yet, say "assumed" out loud and write down the number at which the assumption would change the design.

The line you never cross

Never present an estimate without a sanity check against something real.

Every estimate should be held up against a system that already exists — one you run, or one whose scale is public knowledge. "Six thousand reads a second" means little on its own; "about what our current API does at lunchtime" or "a hundredth of a large social network" gives it a place in the world. If the estimate says a photo app for ten million people needs less storage than your laptop has, the estimate is wrong, and the check is the only thing that would have told you.

Where the estimate shows up

Four rooms where the same minute of arithmetic is the difference between an opinion and an answer.

  • The interview — three minutes of the forty-fiveSay the inputs aloud, round hard, keep the units. Interviewers are not checking your multiplication; they are checking that you know which of the four outputs matters.
  • The design reviewThe capacity paragraph. A reviewer who cannot find it assumes the boxes on the diagram were not earned — because without the numbers, they were not.
  • The launch-readiness meeting"Will it hold?" answered as: which of the four is closest to its limit, by how much, and what we do if the peak is three times what we assumed.
  • The cloud billStorage a month and egress a month are the estimate's last two lines with a currency attached. The arithmetic that finds the hard part also finds the expensive part, and they are usually the same line.

In productionA month after launch, put the estimate next to the graphs. Each of the four numbers will be off by some factor. The factor is the most valuable thing you learn that quarter, because it is the correction you apply to every estimate after it.

04See it

Estimate any system in a minute

The knobs are the inputs — users, what each does, how big each thing is, how long it is kept. The four numbers underneath are the outputs, already multiplied by three for peak. The list is the working, one line per number, so that every result can be traced back to an input. At the bottom: which of the four is the hard part, and by how much.

Try the four presets. The shortener fits on one machine on every axis. The photo app has an easy request rate and an impossible storage line. The chat app stores almost nothing and writes constantly. The sensor fleet is a system with no readers at all.

    07Take away

    Five questions worth asking

    1. "What are we assuming? Let's write the inputs down first."
      Turns a guess into an estimate in one move. Users, actions per user, size per action — three numbers, each labelled measured or assumed, and now everyone in the room is multiplying the same things.
    2. "Per day, or per second?"
      The unit question, asked every time a rate is mentioned. The factor between them is a hundred thousand, and it has been lost in more meetings than anyone will admit.
    3. "And at peak — three times that?"
      No day is flat. Sizing for the average is sizing for the day that never happens; the launch, the sale and the news story are the days the system is actually for.
    4. "Which of the four is closest to its limit — reads, writes, bytes, or bandwidth?"
      The question the whole exercise exists to answer. One of the four is the hard part; the other three are not worth a diagram box until the numbers say so.
    5. "Does that match anything we already run?"
      The sanity check. An estimate that cannot be placed next to a real system — yours or a public one — is a number with no way to be wrong, which is the same as no number.
