What one request costs · Lesson 05

Nines, and what “consistent” actually means

Two words end most design arguments before they start: "available" and "consistent". Both sound like things you simply want more of. Both have exact meanings, exact prices, and a theorem that says during the one moment it matters, you cannot have all of the second and keep the first.

01Ground floor

How much is a nine, and who is it a promise to

TAPAny word with a dotted underline opens a plain explanation in English and Vietnamese, plus where that thing shows up once a system is actually running. Nothing here assumes you have met it before.

"The system should be highly available" is not a requirement, for the reason lesson 01 gave: it carries no number. The number is a count of nines. 99% available sounds excellent and is a disaster — it allows three and a half days of downtime a year. 99.9% — three nines — is about nine hours a year, or 43 minutes a month. 99.99% is under an hour a year. 99.999% — five nines, the figure people reach for without thinking — is five minutes a year, and buying it is roughly ten times harder and more expensive than the nine below it.

That measurement has three names, depending on who is being spoken to. The raw number — the fraction of requests that succeed — is an , the indicator. The target you hold yourself to — "99.9% over 30 days" — is an , the objective, an internal promise the team designs against. The same figure written into a customer contract with money attached, credits paid out if you miss it, is an . The SLA is always set a little looser than the SLO, so that missing the target warns the team before it refunds the customer.

And the nine has a mirror image that is more useful than the nine itself: the . If the target is 99.9%, then 0.1% — about 43 minutes a month — is a budget of downtime you are allowed to spend, on risky deploys, on experiments, on the incident that was always going to happen. It reframes reliability from a wish into an amount. A team with budget left can move fast; a team that has spent it slows down and stops shipping until the month resets. The nine you promised and the risk you can take are the same number, read from two ends.

99% — two nines
~3.65 days of downtime a year. Fine for an internal tool; a catastrophe for anything a customer pays for.
99.9% — three nines
~8.8 hours a year, ~43 minutes a month. The common target for a serious product, and the budget most SLOs are written at.
99.99% — four nines
~53 minutes a year. Now every dependency and every deploy has to be careful; the cost step is real.
99.999% — five nines
~5 minutes a year. Rarely needed, often demanded, and each nine above costs about ten times the one below.

In productionThe SLO is the number a launch-readiness meeting is really asking about, and the error budget is the number that should decide whether this week's risky change ships now or waits. A team that tracks neither is not being more relaxed about reliability; it is being reliable by luck, and finding out the target only after it has been missed.

02The idea

Availability is a chain, and consistency is three words in a coat

The first trap in the nines is that they do not add — they multiply, downward. A request that has to pass through ten services to be answered is only available if all ten are up at once. Ten services at three nines each is not three nines end to end; it is 0.999 to the tenth power, about 99.0% — a whole nine gone, from a chain where every single link met its target. This is why an end-to-end SLO forces every dependency's SLO higher than the promise: to deliver three nines through five services, each of the five needs closer to four. Every box you added in lesson 01 was also a subtraction from the availability you can promise, and nobody feels it until the chain is long.

The second word is worse, because it hides three different meanings under one coat. When a developer says a system is "consistent" they might mean any of: every reader sees the latest write the instant it happens (strong); readers might see an old value for a moment but all copies will agree soon (); or a single user at least sees their own writes even if others lag. These are not degrees of the same thing — they are different promises with different costs, and most consistency arguments are two people using the one word for two of them.

Which brings the two words into collision. Your data lives on more than one machine — it has to, for the availability above. Normally both copies agree and you have consistency and availability together. Then, rarely and briefly, the network between the copies breaks: a . Both machines are alive; the wire between them is not. A write lands on the side a user can reach. Do you accept it — and let the two copies disagree until the wire heals — or refuse it, so nothing is ever wrong but this user cannot write? That is the whole of the , and it is not a menu of three. It is a forced choice between two, made only in the seconds the network is split.

You do not choose CAP on a whiteboard. The network chooses the moment for you; all you decide in advance is which way you will fall.

Refuse the write and you have chosen — consistency during the partition. Users on the cut-off side get errors when they try to write, for as long as the split lasts, and in exchange no one anywhere ever reads a wrong value; when the wire heals there is nothing to reconcile, because nothing moved. That is the right default for anything where two truths is worse than a pause: money, inventory, a seat on a flight. Accept the write and you have chosen — availability during the partition. No one gets an error, writes are taken on whichever copy is reachable, and the price is that the copies disagree until the wire heals and then have to be merged — most fold together, a few genuinely collide and need a rule or a person to resolve. That is the right default for a like button, a status page, a feed, a cart you would rather keep than lose.

One refinement worth knowing by name, because interviewers ask for it: the choice does not only exist during a partition. Even when the network is perfectly healthy, keeping copies strongly consistent costs latency — every write has to wait for the other copies to confirm. That extension is PACELC: during a Partition, choose A or C; Else, in normal running, choose between Latency and Consistency. The same tension, present every day in small print, not only in the rare emergency. CAP is the headline; PACELC is the fuller sentence.

In productionEvery distributed database has already made this choice, and its documentation states it — usually on a page titled something like "behaviour during a network partition". Postgres with synchronous replication leans CP; Cassandra and DynamoDB lean AP and hand you conflict resolution; many let you choose per operation. Reading that page is how you find out what your database will do in the emergency, before the emergency.

03The danger

The nines you promised the wrong way

Availability and consistency are where good intentions cost the most, because "more" sounds free and is not. Each of these is a real decision dressed as an obvious one.

Buying nines you do not need

Somebody says "let's aim for five nines" and it sounds like ambition. It is a bill. Five nines means five minutes of downtime a year, which means no single point of failure anywhere, multi-region everything, and an on-call culture that treats a five-minute blip as a crisis — an order of magnitude more expensive than the four nines almost every product actually needs. The honest first question is not "how available can we be" but "what does an hour of downtime actually cost us", and for most systems the answer makes three or four nines the correct, and much cheaper, target.

Promising an SLO you cannot make, because your dependencies won't

A team promises 99.9% and builds on five services that each promise 99.9%. The arithmetic already lost: five links at three nines is about 99.5% end to end, and that is before the team's own code fails once. An SLO is only real if every dependency underneath it is higher, and a promise made without checking the chain is a promise the multiplication has already broken. You cannot be more available than the things you depend on, added up the multiplying way.

Choosing CP or AP by accident

The most expensive version of this is not choosing at all — taking whatever the database does by default and finding out which it was during the incident. A payment system that turns out to be AP sells the same thing twice when the network blips. A social feed that turns out to be CP goes down entirely because two datacenters could not talk for four seconds, to protect data that nobody would have minded being a moment stale. Both are the right mechanism attached to the wrong kind of data. The choice is per feature, not per company: the same system should usually be CP for its ledger and AP for its "who's online" indicator.

The line you never cross

Never let a like button take the whole site down to stay consistent, and never let money stay available by disagreeing with itself.

Sort every piece of data by one question: is a brief disagreement, healed in seconds, worse than a brief outage? For money, inventory and bookings the answer is yes — choose CP and accept that a partition means some writes are refused. For likes, views, presence, feeds and drafts the answer is no — choose AP and accept that a partition means some reads are stale and a few edits must be merged. Getting this backwards is not a small mistake; it is either lost money or a self-inflicted outage, and both are found in production during the one rare minute the network is split.

Where the choice shows up

Four rooms where "available" and "consistent" stop being adjectives and start being decisions with a price.

  • The SLO reviewHow many nines, measured how, over what window — and what the error budget buys the team the freedom to do. The number, not the adjective.
  • The dependency mapEvery service your request touches, with its own SLO, multiplied. The end-to-end number is always smaller than the smallest link, and usually smaller than people expect.
  • The database choiceIts partition behaviour is a fixed property you are opting into. Read the page that says what it does when a replica cannot be reached — lessons 08 and 16 go deeper.
  • The incident review"We were down because two regions could not talk" is a CP system meeting a partition. "We charged twice" is an AP system meeting one. The postmortem is where an accidental choice becomes a visible one.

In productionThe worst version of the consistency choice is the one nobody made: the default was accepted, the data's real need was never asked, and the mismatch surfaces as either double-charged customers or an outage over a four-second network blip. The choice is cheap to make on purpose and expensive to discover by accident.

07Take away

Five questions worth asking

  1. "How many nines, and what does an hour of downtime actually cost us?"
    Turns "highly available" into a number, and the number into a budget decision. The cost of downtime is what tells you whether three nines or five is the honest target — and five is almost never it.
  2. "What's our error budget, and how much is left this month?"
    The nine read from the other end. A budget with room means ship the risky change; a spent budget means slow down. It is the single number that should govern how fast a team moves.
  3. "What's the end-to-end availability once you multiply the dependencies?"
    Nines multiply downward. A chain of three-nines services is a two-nines system, and an SLO promised without doing this multiplication is already broken. You cannot be more available than your dependencies, combined.
  4. "When you say consistent — do you mean strong, eventual, or read-your-own-writes?"
    Three different promises, three different costs, one overloaded word. Ask it the first time and you save the argument where two people mean two things and both think they agree.
  5. "During a partition, does this data refuse the write or accept it?"
    The CAP choice, made on purpose. Money refuses and stays correct; a like button accepts and reconciles. The disaster is answering "whatever the database does by default" and finding out during the incident.
